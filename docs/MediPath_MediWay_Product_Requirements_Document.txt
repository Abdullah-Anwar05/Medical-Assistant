Document purpose and instructions to the AI development agent
This document is the authoritative product brief for designing and implementing MediPath and MediWay. Build a secure, accessible, responsive healthcare platform that supports a patient from researching a diagnosis and choosing a suitable specialist through referral, booking, surgery, and recovery. The system must not present itself as an autonomous clinician. Clinical decision support, imaging interpretation, urgency classification, and treatment recommendations require an appropriately governed clinical workflow and human validation.
The initial product concept is UK/NHS-aware because it references NHS hospitals, NHS trusts, National Joint Registry (NJR) information, NHS e-Referral Service (e-RS), and UK clinical safety standards. Treat the UK as the reference implementation, not as a confirmed exclusive launch territory. Before deployment in another country (including Pakistan), replace or extend legal, clinical, provider-directory, identity, interoperability, and reimbursement integrations for that jurisdiction.

1. Product vision, goals and non-goals
1.1 Vision
Create an end-to-end digital surgical pathway platform connecting patients, clinicians, surgeons, secretaries, hospitals and rehabilitation services. Patients should be able to understand their options and preferences, compare relevant providers using transparent evidence, manage their records, and see what happens next. Clinical teams should be able to receive structured information, reduce repetitive administration, coordinate referrals and bookings, and monitor pathway progress.
1.2 Product goals
Make it easier for a patient to find an appropriate surgeon and hospital for a specific diagnosis or procedure.
Collect a structured patient profile, including symptoms, conditions, medical history, medications, allergies, prior treatment, referrals and imaging.
Present surgeon and hospital information with provenance, date, context, limitations and meaningful comparisons.
Support a transparent, personalised roadmap from referral to consultation, pre-operative assessment, surgery, discharge and rehabilitation.
Provide 3D pre-operative and post-operative anatomical visualisation, clearly distinguishing patient-specific reconstruction from illustrative models.
Reduce avoidable administrative work through workflow automation and authorised integrations.
Support outcome measurement and patient experience collection after treatment.
1.3 Non-goals and prohibited assumptions
Do not autonomously diagnose, decide surgical eligibility, prescribe treatment, or make final urgency decisions.
Do not guarantee surgery dates or waiting times unless a live, authoritative provider scheduling integration confirms them.
Do not invent surgeon outcome statistics, infection rates, satisfaction scores, testimonials, or availability.
Do not infer religion, ethnicity, language, or other sensitive characteristics. Preferences must be voluntary, patient-controlled, and used only for appropriate service matching.
Do not claim that a 2D X-ray alone produces an exact 3D anatomical reconstruction.
Do not rank surgeons using a single simplistic score or unadjusted outcome metric.

4. Functional requirements
4.1 Identity, account and access
Support patient account creation using verified email and/or mobile number; provide secure sign-in and account recovery.
Support appropriate identity assurance for clinical record access. Do not assume that a QR/barcode scan proves identity.
Support authorised carers or proxies with explicit delegation, scope, expiry and revocation.
Implement role-based access control (RBAC), organisation scoping and least-privilege access.
Maintain access logs for viewing, downloading, editing, sharing and exporting sensitive records.
Support patient consent/preferences for data sharing, marketing, testimonials and research as separate purposes.

4.3 Imaging and document management
Accept common document formats and diagnostic imaging formats. Prioritise DICOM for imaging; support PDF and standard image formats for reports and external uploads.
Retain original uploaded files unchanged, record checksum, source, uploader, date/time, modality and associated body region/laterality.
Provide secure image/document viewing, upload progress, file validation, malware scanning and clear failure messages.
Support DICOM series and metadata, with a standards-based imaging viewer where feasible.
Link each scan to the relevant encounter, diagnosis, anatomical region and clinical review.
Allow authorised clinicians to annotate or add structured findings without overwriting the original image or report.
Show when a scan is incomplete, low quality, incorrectly labelled or not suitable for a requested analysis.
5. MediView 3D: pre-operative and post-operative modelling
MediView 3D is a major differentiating feature. It must support patient education and clinician review while clearly communicating model provenance and limitations. The initial target use case is knee osteoarthritis and total knee arthroplasty, with an extensible architecture for hip and other orthopaedic procedures.
5.1 Pre-operative 3D model
Generate a patient-specific 3D bone model from suitable imaging, preferably CT for detailed bone geometry. MRI may contribute soft-tissue context where appropriate.
Where only 2D X-rays are available, provide 2D measurements and/or a clearly labelled estimated visualisation only. Do not present an inferred model as an exact reconstruction.
Support segmentation of relevant anatomy (femur, tibia, patella where appropriate), subject to validated algorithm performance and clinician review.
Allow rotate, pan, zoom, reset view, anatomical orientation, layer visibility and side-by-side comparison.
Provide optional overlays for osteophytes, joint-space narrowing, bone loss, deformity and alignment measurements where supported by the modality and validated method.
Show measurement units, reference method, source image date, model generation date, confidence/quality indicator and clinician sign-off status.
Allow surgeon annotations and planning notes to be saved as a separate versioned record.
5.2 Post-operative 3D model and implant in situ
Create a post-operative model from appropriate post-operative imaging and implant geometry/records. Do not imply actual implant positioning from a generic stock model.
Display femoral component, tibial component, polyethylene insert and patellar component only where relevant and supported by the procedure and records.
Capture implant manufacturer, model, catalogue/reference number, size, lot/serial/UDI where available, side, fixation type and operative date.
Allow comparison between pre-operative anatomy, planned model (if available), and post-operative reconstruction.
Provide an implant passport that links device details, surgery, hospital, surgeon, imaging and follow-up.
Clearly label any educational animation or generic prosthesis as illustrative rather than patient-specific.

6. Diagnosis and procedure discovery
Provide searchable diagnosis and procedure catalogue with synonyms, plain-language descriptions and clinician-facing terminology.
Example journey: patient selects osteoarthritis of the knee, indicates side and symptoms, and explores total knee arthroplasty (knee replacement) as a potential treatment option.
Do not imply that every diagnosis requires surgery. Present conservative and alternative treatment information where clinically reviewed.
Allow filters for specialty, procedure, postcode/radius, provider type (NHS/private), hospital, accessibility, language and appointment availability.
Display educational content, typical pathway stages, questions to ask a surgeon and clear advice to seek urgent care for emergency symptoms.

7.3 Comparison and matching logic
The platform may provide a transparent shortlist based on procedure relevance, clinical scope, location, verified data availability and patient preferences. It must not generate a hidden 'best surgeon' score from incomparable or incomplete metrics. Explain why each profile appears and allow the patient to inspect the underlying evidence.
Separate clinical suitability information from convenience/preferences and from paid marketing placements.
Use risk-adjusted outcomes where available; show sample size, confidence/uncertainty, time period and data completeness.
Do not display surgeon-specific infection or complication rates unless a valid, authorised source supports that level of attribution.
Provide clear warnings where a metric is unavailable, not comparable, stale or based on a small sample.
Never use religion or ethnicity as a proxy for clinical quality. If a patient voluntarily records a cultural/religious accommodation preference, use it only to help locate a service that can meet the requested accommodation.

8.1 Urgency bands
The original concept describes urgent, medium and long-wait patients. Implement these as configurable operational categories only after clinical governance approves the definitions, eligibility criteria, escalation routes and service-level targets. Avoid using 'long waiter' as a clinical severity label. A patient's wait estimate must be distinguished from clinical priority.
Conceptual band
System behaviour
Urgent clinical review
Flag to an authorised clinical queue according to a validated protocol; show patient an appropriate status without promising a treatment date.
Routine / standard pathway
Track referral, review, assessment and booking milestones against provider-defined service targets.
Deferred / monitoring
Support clinician-approved monitoring, conservative management and reassessment reminders.
Emergency symptoms must trigger a clear emergency-care instruction appropriate to the launch country. The app must not place emergency cases into a routine elective waiting list.

10. Digital patient ID / QR or barcode
Assign each patient a unique, non-meaningful identifier and display a QR code or barcode in the app.
The code must not contain readable personal or medical information. It should resolve to a secure, short-lived or access-controlled lookup.
Scanning must require appropriate staff authentication and patient identity checks; scanning alone must not reveal the record.
Support check-in, encounter association, referral tracking and authorised document retrieval.
Allow code rotation/revocation and log every scan/lookup.
11. Patient testimonials, reviews and marketing profiles
Allow patients to submit feedback after a defined care milestone, with separate consent for public display.
Clearly label verified experience, unverified comment, invited review and provider-supplied marketing content.
Moderate for privacy, defamation, identifying information, medical misinformation and inappropriate promotional claims.
Do not imply that an individual testimonial predicts another patient's outcome.
Provide a correction/removal process and retain audit evidence of moderation.
Clearly label paid placements or sponsored profile enhancements; never let payment influence clinical matching or outcome scores.
12. Secretary referral automation and workflow management
Create tasks automatically when a referral arrives, when a required document is missing, when a clinician requests more information, or when a patient needs an appointment.
Provide secretary/administrator work queues with filters by provider, specialty, status, urgency flag, due date and owner.
Allow authorised staff to request documents, contact patients, book/reschedule appointments and update administrative milestones.
Maintain message templates and reminders approved by the provider.
Escalate overdue tasks and failed integrations to designated operational queues.
Do not allow administrative staff to alter clinical urgency or clinical findings unless their role and governance explicitly permit it.
13. Notifications and communications
In-app notifications for pathway status changes, document requests, appointment reminders and recovery questionnaires.
Email/SMS/push channels configurable by patient preference and consent; avoid sensitive clinical details in lock-screen previews or unsecured channels.
Two-way secure messaging only if resourcing, response expectations, clinical escalation and record retention are defined.
Every clinically meaningful message should be stored in or linked to the appropriate patient record.
14. Recovery, outcomes and implant passport
Collect patient-reported pain, mobility, function, satisfaction and quality-of-life measures at clinically approved timepoints.
Support rehabilitation milestones, exercise/physiotherapy plans and appointment reminders authored or approved by the care team.
Provide symptom check-ins with safe escalation instructions; do not use a generic chatbot as a substitute for urgent clinical assessment.
Maintain a longitudinal record of operation, implant components, device identifiers, discharge, imaging and follow-up.
Allow patients to download/share a structured summary with authorised clinicians.

17. Security, privacy and governance requirements
Encrypt data in transit and at rest; manage keys securely and rotate credentials.
Use least privilege, role-based and organisation-scoped access; enforce MFA for privileged and clinical users.
Complete a Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) before processing patient health data at scale.
Document lawful basis and Article 9 condition for special-category health data; do not assume consent is the only or always the correct legal basis.
Define controller/processor roles, retention schedules, data-subject rights, breach response, international transfer safeguards and subcontractor controls.
Keep immutable or tamper-evident audit records for access and changes to clinical information.
Support data minimisation, patient transparency, correction workflows and secure deletion/retention according to law and clinical obligations.
Perform penetration testing, vulnerability management, secure SDLC, dependency scanning, backup/restore testing and incident response exercises.
Separate production, test and development environments; never use identifiable patient data in development unless explicitly authorised and protected.
Use de-identified or synthetic datasets for development and model evaluation wherever possible.
18. Clinical safety, medical device and AI governance
The regulatory status depends on intended purpose and functionality. A directory, booking tool and administrative workflow may have a different regulatory profile from software that analyses imaging, estimates disease severity, recommends treatment or assigns urgency. The product owner must obtain specialist regulatory advice and document intended purpose, claims, risk classification and conformity route before clinical deployment.
Appoint a Clinical Safety Officer and establish a clinical risk management system.
Assess applicability of NHS DCB0129 (manufacturer) and DCB0160 (deploying healthcare organisation) for the UK deployment.
Maintain a clinical hazard log, risk controls, clinical safety case/report and change-control process.
Assess whether imaging analysis, segmentation, clinical prioritisation or treatment decision support qualifies as Software as a Medical Device (SaMD)/AI as a Medical Device under MHRA rules.
Validate AI models on representative, appropriately governed datasets; document sensitivity, specificity, calibration, subgroup performance, failure modes and intended-use limits.
Monitor drift and performance after deployment; maintain model versioning, rollback and incident reporting.
Require clinician review and sign-off for consequential clinical outputs; make the provenance of AI-generated content visible.
Provide a safe fallback when AI, imaging processing or integration is unavailable.

20. Accessibility, language and patient-centred design
Offer plain-language explanations of diagnoses, procedures, outcomes and waiting-time concepts.
Support multiple interface languages and verified clinician language profiles; provide interpreter booking where available.
Allow patients to request accessibility accommodations, larger text, screen-reader support and alternative communication channels.
Use inclusive, non-stigmatising language and avoid assuming that all patients want surgery.
Make personal preferences optional and explain how each preference affects matching.
Ensure every important status is communicated with text/icon and not colour alone.

As a clinician, I want to review a referral and imaging.

24. Key risks and mitigations
Risk
Mitigation
Incorrect AI imaging or urgency output
Human validation, defined intended use, validation datasets, uncertainty display, clinical safety controls and escalation.
Misleading surgeon comparisons
Source transparency, risk adjustment, cohort/sample-size display, no composite score without validation, clear hospital vs surgeon attribution.
Out-of-date availability
Live integration where possible; timestamp all estimates; distinguish indicative from confirmed.
Data breach or inappropriate access
Encryption, MFA, RBAC, audit, DPIA, penetration testing, incident response and least privilege.
Integration partner unavailable
Manual safe workflow, reconciliation queues, clear data provenance and no false live status.
3D model mistaken for exact anatomy
Modality/provenance labels, model confidence, clinician sign-off and explicit estimated/illustrative tags.
Unconsented testimonials
Separate consent, moderation, removal process and public-display controls.
Regulatory scope creep
Intended-purpose control, change assessment and regulatory review before enabling new clinical claims.

28. Instructions for the AI implementation agent
Treat this document as the baseline product specification. Convert each requirement into epics, user stories, technical tasks and test cases.
Before coding, produce: system architecture, threat model, data-flow diagram, role/permission matrix, data dictionary, API contracts, integration plan, design system and phased delivery plan.
Identify assumptions and unresolved decisions explicitly. Do not silently invent clinical protocols, provider data, regulatory approvals or integration credentials.
Use synthetic demo data in prototypes. Clearly mark all fictional surgeons, hospitals, outcomes, wait times, testimonials, patient identities and surgery dates as demo content.
Build the MVP without autonomous diagnosis or urgency decisions. Gate advanced AI and patient-specific 3D clinical functionality behind validation, governance and regulatory approval.
Implement auditability, accessibility, privacy and clinical safety from the beginning rather than as later enhancements.
Create automated unit, integration, security, accessibility and end-to-end tests. Include negative tests for wrong-patient linkage, stale data, missing consent, unauthorised access, failed integrations and uncertain AI outputs.
Do not expose the QR code as a bearer credential or encode protected health information in it.
Do not fabricate public surgeon metrics. If an authoritative data feed is unavailable, show 'data unavailable' and provide a link/source explanation.
Deliver a clickable prototype first, followed by the Phase 1 MVP, and seek product-owner and clinical sign-off at each gate.
Appendix A — Suggested initial demo scenario
Use a synthetic patient with knee osteoarthritis who is exploring total knee arthroplasty. The patient registers, completes a medical history and symptom questionnaire, uploads a fictional X-ray and referral letter, and receives a clearly labelled demo pre-operative knee model. The patient searches hospitals by postcode, compares two fictional surgeon profiles using sourced/labelled demo metrics, requests a consultation, and sees an example roadmap. A clinician user reviews the referral, records a decision and updates the pathway. After a simulated procedure, the patient sees a clearly labelled illustrative post-operative knee replacement model and a fictional implant passport. The patient then completes recovery check-ins. All content must be labelled DEMO and must not be mistaken for real clinical advice, real provider availability or an actual patient's record.
